# What is a mortgage?

A mortgage is a loan secured by real property. The borrower repays principal and interest under the note, and the security instrument gives the lender rights in the property if the loan is not repaid as agreed. Loan terms, costs, and protections depend on the product and transaction.

In everyday terms, a lender provides money to buy or refinance a home, and the borrower agrees to repay it over time. The borrower owns the home, while the home serves as collateral for the loan. If the borrower does not repay the loan as agreed, the lender may pursue foreclosure through the legally required process.

# Why lenders charge interest

Interest is the cost of borrowing money. It compensates the lender and the investors who supply funds for making that money available and taking on risk. The principal is the amount borrowed; interest is the additional amount charged over time. A mortgage's cost can include both the interest paid over time and certain costs paid at closing.

# What is included in a mortgage payment?

A monthly housing payment may include principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, flood insurance, and other required items. Condominium or homeowners-association dues are usually paid separately but are commonly considered when qualifying. The exact payment must come from a current loan estimate or reviewed scenario.

# Fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages

With a fixed-rate mortgage, the note rate does not change during the loan term, although the total payment can change when taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, or escrow requirements change. An adjustable-rate mortgage has an initial rate period followed by adjustments based on the note's index, margin, adjustment schedule, and caps. The initial ARM rate should not be treated as the permanent payment.

# Interest rate and APR

The interest rate is the percentage used to calculate interest on the loan balance. Annual percentage rate, or APR, is a disclosure measure that incorporates the interest rate and certain finance charges. APR can help compare similar offers, but it is not the note rate and does not include every possible ownership cost.

Some closing charges are treated as prepaid finance charges because they are paid to obtain the financing. Other expenses, such as homeowners insurance, pay for a separate product and generally are not treated the same way in the APR calculation. Which charges are included, along with other assumptions used in the calculation, can affect the disclosed APR.

APR is useful, but the lowest APR is not automatically the best deal. Two quotes can use different fee assumptions, and a small APR difference may not show which option best fits the borrower's plans. A better comparison reviews the note rate, lender credits or discount points, and itemized costs side by side for the same loan scenario.

# Discount points and lender credits

One discount point equals 1% of the loan amount. Paying discount points generally means paying more at closing for a lower interest rate. A lender credit generally reduces upfront closing costs in exchange for a higher rate. The value of either choice depends on the exact pricing and how long the borrower expects to keep the loan; the assistant must not recommend a choice or calculate a break-even result from chat data.

# Where mortgage rates come from

Mortgage rates are strongly influenced by investor demand for mortgage-backed securities, or MBS. Many mortgages with similar features are grouped into securities that can be bought and sold in the bond market. The prices investors are willing to pay for those securities influence the pricing lenders can offer on new mortgages.

Mortgage-backed securities often move in the same general direction as the broader bond market, including U.S. Treasury securities, but mortgage pricing does not move in perfect lockstep with any single Treasury yield. Mortgages have different risks and behaviors, including the possibility that homeowners sell or refinance and repay a loan earlier than an investor expected.

At the individual-loan level, lenders also adjust pricing for the loan program and risk characteristics supported by the transaction. These can include credit profile, down payment or equity, occupancy, property type, loan purpose, loan amount, and other program factors. This is why two borrowers—or two different loan scenarios for the same borrower—may not receive the same pricing.

# Why mortgage rates change during the day

Rates can change daily and sometimes more than once in a day. Moment-to-moment changes are usually driven by movement in mortgage-backed securities as investors react to economic data, inflation expectations, Federal Reserve communication, government borrowing, geopolitical events, and changes in the outlook for growth and risk.

When demand for mortgage-backed securities rises and their prices improve, lenders may be able to offer better mortgage pricing. When those security prices fall, lenders may need to charge more. A pricing change may appear as a different note rate, different upfront points or lender credits, or a combination of both.

Lender operations matter too. A lender may adjust its pricing based on funding capacity, staffing, loan volume, competitive goals, or desired profit margin. These business differences help explain why lenders can quote different prices at the same moment even though they are responding to the same market.

The assistant must not quote a current market rate from this general knowledge. A current quote requires live pricing for a defined loan scenario.

# Rate locks

A rate lock is a lender commitment to hold specified pricing for a stated period subject to the lock terms and unchanged application details. Lock periods expire, extensions may cost money, and pricing may change if the loan or borrower information changes. A rate lock is different from a prequalification or approval.

A lock identifies the agreed note rate and related pricing and sets a deadline by which the loan generally must close and fund. Available lock periods vary by lender and transaction. Common windows may include roughly 30, 45, or 60 days, with shorter or longer options sometimes available.

Longer locks commonly cost more than shorter locks for the same rate because the lender is taking market risk for a longer period. That difference may appear as higher discount points, a smaller lender credit, or different rate pricing. The best lock period is usually long enough to cover the expected loan process with a reasonable cushion, without paying for substantially more time than the transaction needs.

# Locking versus floating

Locking protects the agreed pricing from later market increases during the lock period, subject to the lender's terms. Floating means the pricing is not yet locked, so it may improve or worsen before the borrower locks. There is no universal answer about the best day to lock because future market movement cannot be known in advance.

A practical question is whether the borrower is comfortable accepting a higher rate or cost if the market worsens. If worse pricing could threaten the desired payment, qualification, purchase contract, refinance benefit, or willingness to complete the transaction, that risk deserves serious consideration. Adam or his team can explain the current choices and lock terms, but the assistant must not predict rates or direct a visitor to lock or float.

# Lock expirations and extensions

If a loan will not close before its lock expires, the lender may offer an extension. Availability, cost, and responsibility for that cost depend on the lender's policy and the reason for the delay. An extension can involve added points or reduced lender credit, and the cost may be more significant when market rates have risen since the original lock.

Some lenders may use pricing rules that compare the original locked terms with current market pricing after an expiration or material change. Borrowers should review the lock confirmation, expiration date, extension policy, and any conditions that could change the pricing. A lock does not remove the need to provide documents, satisfy loan conditions, or close on time.

# Loan term and amortization

The loan term is the scheduled repayment period. Amortization is the process of reducing principal through scheduled payments. A shorter term often produces a higher required principal-and-interest payment and less total interest than a longer term at the same balance and rate, but actual pricing and costs vary.

# Prequalification and preapproval

These terms are not used identically by every lender. A prequalification may be based on preliminary or unverified information. A preapproval generally involves a more complete application and review, but it is still conditional and is not a final loan approval or assurance of closing. Property, documentation, credit, program, and underwriting conditions remain.
