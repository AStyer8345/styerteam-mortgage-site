Bank Statement Loans in Austin and Across Texas

By Adam Styer, Senior Loan Officer • NMLS #513013 • Company NMLS #2072896

If your tax returns show too little income for the mortgage you want, a bank statement loan may help. Depending on the program, the lender can use eligible deposits from 12 or 24 months of personal or business bank statements to calculate your self-employment income.

The first thing I’d ask is where your customers pay you. That helps me figure out which statements to review and what the lender may be able to count.

When a bank statement loan is worth a look

If you own a business, work in the trades, or get paid on 1099s, this option may be worth a look when your deposits are steady and the tax-return calculation falls short. It can be used for a purchase or refinance, depending on the program and property.

I’d also check whether your tax returns can support a conventional loan. Some deductions, such as eligible depreciation, can be added back during that review. If both paths work, compare the monthly payment, fees, and cash you’d need at closing. A bank statement loan can cost more, so it’s worth seeing what the flexibility would cost on your file.

Which deposits count toward mortgage income?

Business statements. The lender starts with eligible business receipts, sets aside borrowed funds, and traces transfers so the same payment is counted only once. The program determines how business expenses and your share of ownership affect the income calculation.

Personal statements. If you pay yourself from a separate business account, some programs can use those deposits. The lender may ask for statements from both accounts to see where the money came from.

What can complicate a bank statement loan?

  • Mixed accounts or unusual deposits. When customer payments, personal transfers, and borrowed funds land in the same account, the lender has to sort out where the money came from. A large or irregular payment may need an invoice or another record that explains it.
  • A newer business or changing revenue. A 12-month statement review tells you how far back the lender looks at deposits. How long you’ve been self-employed is a separate requirement. If deposits jumped or dropped recently, be ready to explain what changed.
  • Business expenses. Some programs accept a letter from a qualified tax preparer to support a different expense calculation. Before you ask for a letter, I’d check what the lender accepts and what the letter needs to show.

The lender also reviews your credit, monthly debts, and the property. For a purchase, it checks your down payment. For a refinance, it checks your equity. Some programs require cash left over after closing.

A real refinance where one deposit mattered

A self-employed homeowner with 1099 income wanted to refinance to recover money spent on home repairs and pay off cards used during the renovation. The underwriter initially excluded a payment described as a bonus, which lowered the income used to qualify. The review included records from the payer and a CPA letter documenting business expenses.

The refinance funded using 12 months of personal bank statements. The proceeds reimbursed the repair spending and paid off the related card balances.

What to send for a first look

Rough numbers are fine. Tell me:

  • Whether you’re buying or refinancing, the Texas city or area, and the price or estimated value.
  • What your business does, how long you’ve been doing it, and how much of it you own.
  • Where customers pay you, roughly how much comes in each month, and whether deposits vary by season.
  • Anything that’s driving the decision, like a prior lender’s answer, a down payment budget, or a closing deadline.

I’ll tell you which income calculation I’d check first and what might get in the way. If we need statements, we’ll give you a secure way to send them. Leave account numbers and financial documents out of the form.
