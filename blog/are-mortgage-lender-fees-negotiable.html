Which Mortgage Lender Fees Are Negotiable?

By Adam Styer | NMLS #513013 | Published April 12, 2026 | Updated November 18, 2026

Some lender-charged mortgage fees may be negotiable, but no fee reduction is guaranteed. Third-party charges are generally harder to change, and government charges are not controlled by the lender. Compare the complete Loan Estimate—rate, points, lender credits, services, total closing costs, cash to close, and loan terms—not Section A alone.

The CFPB says you may negotiate mortgage terms and costs until signing, but the lender or seller may refuse a requested change. Lender-charged fees are usually easier to negotiate than third-party fees.

I'm Adam Styer, a mortgage broker in Austin, Texas, NMLS #513013. The useful question is not simply whether a charge is “negotiable.” It is who controls the charge, what changes elsewhere in the offer, and whether the revised loan is actually better for your expected time in the home.

Which Mortgage Fees May Be Negotiable?

Start with lender-charged items and ask what service each charge covers. The CFPB specifically recommends asking for justification when a lender charges underwriting and processing fees and asking whether a lender fee can be waived or reduced.

  • Origination, underwriting, and processing charges — Ask what each charge covers and whether the lender will reduce or waive it. A lender may agree, decline, or offset a reduction elsewhere in the pricing.
  • Discount points — Points are prepaid interest used to obtain a particular rate, not automatically an unnecessary fee. Compare the rate, cost, and break-even period against a zero-point or lender-credit option.
  • Rate-lock and application charges — Policies vary. Ask when the charge applies, whether it is refundable, and whether an alternative rate or lock period changes it.

A lower lender charge is useful only if the rest of the offer remains competitive. Recheck the interest rate, points, lender credits, prepayment terms, mortgage insurance, cash to close, and five-year cost after any revision.

Which Charges Are Harder to Change?

Third-party and government charges should not be described as one universally “fixed” category. Control and shopping rights differ by item and transaction.

  • Appraisal, credit-report, tax-service, and flood-certification fees — These are commonly paid to third parties and are generally harder to negotiate. Ask for an explanation and compare the disclosed amount.
  • Taxes and recording charges — Government authorities impose these charges; the lender does not set them.
  • Texas title-policy premium — Texas regulates the policy premium, so licensed title companies charge the same premium for the same policy amount. Escrow, delivery, tax-certificate, and other closing service fees can still differ, and the buyer and seller may negotiate who pays the policy premium.
  • Prepaid interest, property taxes, insurance, and initial escrow deposits — These are not lender origination fees. They depend on timing, coverage, tax obligations, and the escrow analysis.

Sections B and C identify services you cannot and can shop for, while other sections show government charges, prepaids, escrow, lender credits, and cash to close. Review the entire form. If you want a line-by-line orientation, use the guide on how to read a Loan Estimate.

How Do You Compare and Negotiate Fees?

Ask for current Loan Estimates for the same transaction and compare them before committing to a lender.

  1. Hold the scenario constant. Use the same loan type, property, purchase price, down payment, credit assumptions, lock period, and occupancy.
  2. Compare estimates issued close together. Market pricing changes, so offers from different dates may not be comparable.
  3. Compare the whole offer. Review the rate, points, lender credits, Sections A through J, cash to close, and page-three comparisons.
  4. Ask precise questions. Request an explanation for each difference and ask whether a lender-charged item can be reduced or waived without changing other terms.
  5. Get the revision in writing. Recheck the updated Loan Estimate or other written disclosure before deciding.

A lower fee can come with a higher rate or smaller lender credit. The right comparison depends on total cost, expected holding period, and cash available at closing. Our guide on how to compare two offers side by side walks through that structure.

How Should Broker and Retail Offers Be Compared?

Brokers, banks, credit unions, and direct lenders can use different compensation and pricing structures. None is automatically the least expensive for every borrower or every day.

Compare written offers using the same assumptions:

  • Rate and lock period — Confirm the same rate type, lock length, and pricing date.
  • Points and credits — Separate borrower-paid points from lender credits and other origination charges.
  • Total costs and cash to close — Include third-party services, prepaids, escrow, and any seller or lender credits.
  • Loan terms and service — Consider mortgage insurance, prepayment terms, product fit, documentation, and execution risk—not price alone.

A broker comparison should show the available lender choices and disclosed compensation for the actual scenario. It should not rely on a blanket claim that wholesale pricing always beats a bank.

What If a Lender Will Not Change a Fee?

A lender can refuse a requested change. Ask for the reason and decide using the complete written offer.

  • Ask which charges are lender-controlled, third-party, government-imposed, prepaid, or escrowed.
  • Ask whether a lower fee changes the rate, points, lender credits, or lock period.
  • Compare another current offer if the explanation or total cost is not competitive.
  • Do not rely on a verbal concession; review the revised written disclosure.
