How Lenders Review Bank Statements for a Texas Mortgage

By Adam Styer | NMLS #513013 | Published June 30, 2026 · Updated September 17, 2026

A bank-statement mortgage review asks which deposits are eligible income, what expenses apply, and how much of the business income belongs to you. This guide explains that review and the records that make it clearer. For the main program comparison, costs, and next steps, see Bank Statement Loans in Texas.

Customer receipts, transfers, borrowed funds, and business expenses need to be separated before that total becomes a useful income estimate.

A useful review also compares the adjusted tax-return calculation. Neither low taxable income nor strong deposits alone establishes which mortgage will fit.

Why Tax Returns and Deposits Can Produce Different Figures

A sole proprietor reports business profit or loss on IRS Schedule C. Mortgage qualifying income can differ from reported net profit after permitted adjustments, including eligible depreciation add-backs and removal of nonrecurring items. The lender must also review earnings history and trends.

Business expenses do not all receive the same underwriting treatment. Use Fannie Mae’s Schedule C cash-flow guidance as a reference for an agency calculation; do not assume every deduction permanently reduces qualifying income dollar for dollar.

The number of returns and required self-employment history depend on the program and file. Fannie Mae’s self-employed borrower guidance addresses those requirements and conditions. An adjusted calculation is needed before comparing tax returns with a deposit-based option.

For example, a restaurant’s deposits and Schedule C profit can differ substantially because of payroll, food costs, rent, and other expenses. Both mortgage methods must account for the appropriate expenses and adjustments. The higher gross-receipts figure alone does not establish repayment ability.

What a Bank Statement Loan Actually Is

The lender identifies eligible deposits and applies the selected program’s calculation. Four parts of the review matter:

  • The statement period. A program may use 12 or 24 consecutive months. Review recent growth, seasonality, or decline across the required period. Statement length is separate from the minimum age of the business; a shorter period does not guarantee more income or better pricing.
  • The accounts involved. The program determines whether personal statements, business statements, or a defined combination can be used. Trace transfers and avoid counting the same earnings in more than one account.
  • Expenses and ownership. Apply the required expense method and ownership treatment. If the program allows an alternative to a standard expense factor, the supporting P&L or expense documentation must meet its requirements; an accountant’s letter alone does not automatically change the result.
  • Excluded deposits. Identify transfers, loan proceeds, refunds, and other non-income deposits. A large customer payment needs context; being unusual does not by itself tell the lender whether it is usable business income.

The result is a documented estimate under a specific program, not a total of all cash entering the account. See the worked deposit calculation on the bank statement page for an illustration with explicit expense and ownership assumptions.

Is This Legal, or Is It a No-Doc Loan?

A bank-statement approach still requires verification. The statements used for income must meet the selected program’s requirements, and additional business, expense, tax, or transaction records may be needed.

For consumer mortgages covered by the federal Ability-to-Repay rule (12 CFR 1026.43), the lender must make the required repayment assessment using appropriate verified information. Account statements are one part of that review; possessing bank statements does not by itself establish that a loan meets every requirement.

A bank-statement program may be offered as a non-qualified mortgage, or Non-QM. That classification depends on the loan and applicable requirements, rather than the absence of a tax-return calculation alone. See the Non-QM loan guide for the broader category and the questions to ask about an offer.

What You'll Actually Need to Qualify

I coach every borrower on the three Cs of loan approval — cash, capacity, and credit. Bank statement loans lean on all three, just with a different income proof:

  • Credit. Review the credit profile and current program requirements together; there is no single score minimum for every bank-statement loan.
  • Cash — down payment and closing costs. The property, loan amount, credit, and program determine the required funds. Compare those needs with other options your file supports.
  • Cash — reserves. Confirm what must remain after closing and which accounts can count. Reserves and funds spent on the transaction are separate requirements.
  • Capacity — supported business income. Explain large receipts, seasonal changes, overdrafts, and transfers. The lender still needs to evaluate expenses, ownership, trends, other debts, and the proposed housing payment.
A useful preparation step: keep records showing which accounts receive customer payments and how money moves between business and personal accounts. Do not move funds just to make an account look stronger; retain the source and transfer documentation.

The Honest Tradeoffs

Before choosing a deposit-based program, compare the practical tradeoffs:

  • Rate and fees. A bank-statement option may cost more than a suitable conventional loan. Compare current written offers using matching assumptions.
  • Funds needed. Compare the required down payment, closing costs, and reserves rather than assuming every program needs the same amount.
  • Documentation. Complete statements for the required period, deposit explanations, and any supporting business or expense records still take preparation.

If adjusted tax-return income supports a suitable loan, compare it fairly with the deposit-based option. Strong deposits do not automatically justify higher costs. Use the self-employed mortgage comparison to choose the path to investigate, and the self-employed mortgage preparation guide to organize the next review.

When a Deposit Review May Be Worthwhile

These situations can justify a closer comparison, rather than establish eligibility:

  • Your adjusted tax-return calculation falls short, and eligible business deposits may support a different calculation.
  • A business change or unusual expense needs explanation under both the tax-return and deposit methods.
  • Recent growth is documented and you want to understand how each program evaluates its sustainability.
  • You earn commissions or contractor income and want to compare statements with eligible 1099 documentation.

If investments are stronger than business income, review qualifying with eligible assets instead. The right comparison depends on your actual qualifying source and the funds needed for the transaction.
