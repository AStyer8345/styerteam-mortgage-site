Just Because One Bank Said No Doesn't Mean You Can't Buy a House

A mortgage denial is a reason to understand your options—and why the first lender said no.

A
Adam Styer
Senior Loan Officer, NMLS #513013 · November 18, 2026

Getting turned down for a mortgage doesn't always mean you can't qualify. Sometimes it just means you haven't found the right lender or loan program.

I've been in the mortgage business long enough to see this happen more times than I can count.

Someone comes to me after being turned down by their bank. They have good credit, make good money, and have plenty of assets. In their mind, getting a mortgage should be pretty straightforward.

But their bank told them no.

And now they're wondering whether they can buy a house at all.

Here's what I tell them: Just because one lender says no doesn't mean every lender will.

Not Every Mortgage Lender Has the Same Guidelines

One of the biggest misconceptions about mortgages is that every lender looks at a loan the same way.

They don't.

Banks, mortgage companies, and investors all have different guidelines, loan programs, and requirements.

A traditional bank might primarily offer conventional loans that follow Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines, along with a limited selection of portfolio products.

There's nothing wrong with those loans. In fact, conventional financing is often the best option when a borrower qualifies.

But not everybody fits neatly into that box.

And that's where having access to different loan programs can make a significant difference.

Self-Employed Borrowers Are a Perfect Example

Let's say you own a successful business.

Your business generates substantial revenue. You have good cash flow, excellent credit, and money in the bank.

But your CPA has done a good job helping you minimize your taxable income through legitimate business deductions.

When you apply for a conventional mortgage, the lender generally starts with your tax returns and calculates qualifying income using specific underwriting rules. Qualifying income is not always the same as taxable income; allowable adjustments depend on the file. Fannie Mae explains its tax-return analysis here.

And suddenly, even though you're making plenty of money, the lender says you don't have enough qualifying income.

I run into this all the time.

The problem isn't necessarily that you can't afford the mortgage. It's that your income doesn't fit the documentation requirements of that particular loan program.

Depending on your situation, there may be other options.

Bank statement loans: Some lenders allow self-employed borrowers to qualify using 12 or 24 months of personal or business bank statements rather than traditional tax-return income calculations.

Profit-and-loss statement loans: Certain non-QM programs may allow qualification using a qualifying business P&L, subject to the lender's documentation and verification requirements.

Asset-based loans: If you have significant eligible assets, there may be ways to use those assets to establish qualifying income, even if your traditional taxable income is relatively low.

These programs aren't necessarily better than conventional financing. They can have higher interest rates, different down payment requirements, and additional restrictions.

But they can provide options that a traditional lender may not offer.

It's Not Just Self-Employed Borrowers

I also see this with retirees, real estate investors, and borrowers who have substantial assets but don't receive a traditional paycheck.

For example, someone might have $2 million in investment accounts, own several properties, and have very little debt.

But because they don't have a traditional W-2 income stream, they run into problems qualifying.

That doesn't automatically mean they can't get a mortgage.

Some conventional and non-QM loan programs have ways to consider eligible assets when determining qualifying income. The rules vary considerably depending on the program. Fannie Mae’s employment-related asset guidelines are one example; not every asset or transaction is eligible.

Real estate investors are another good example.

A borrower may own several rental properties and want to purchase another investment property, but traditional debt-to-income calculations make qualification difficult.

A DSCR loan may be an option for an eligible investment property, not a home you plan to live in.

Instead of relying primarily on the borrower's personal income, a DSCR loan generally evaluates the rental property's income relative to its housing expenses.

Again, it's not the right solution for every borrower or every property. But it's another example of why one lender's answer isn't necessarily the final answer.

Sometimes It's Not the Borrower. It's the Property.

This is another situation I see frequently, particularly here in Texas.

Maybe you're buying a barndominium, a rural property with acreage, or a property that doesn't fit standard conventional lending requirements.

One lender may not finance it at all.

Another might consider it with additional documentation, a different appraisal approach, or a different loan program.

The property still has to meet the applicable lender and investor requirements. Some properties simply aren't eligible for certain financing programs.

But it's worth exploring whether the issue is with the property itself or just that lender's guidelines.

A Second Opinion Doesn't Guarantee an Approval

I want to be clear about something.

There are legitimate reasons mortgages get denied.

Sometimes the debt-to-income ratio is too high. Sometimes there are credit issues, insufficient funds, problems with the property, or income that can't be adequately documented.

And sometimes, after reviewing everything, the answer really is no.

I'm not going to tell someone I can get a loan approved when I don't believe I can.

But I also don't think borrowers should assume they're out of options just because one lender turned them down.

The first thing I want to understand is why the loan was denied.

Was it an income calculation? A property restriction? A lender-specific guideline? Or something that would be a problem regardless of which lender reviewed the file?

Those are very different situations.

And the distinction matters.

Why Working With a Mortgage Broker Can Make a Difference

One of the advantages of working with a mortgage broker is access to multiple lenders and investors.

Instead of being limited to one institution's loan programs, I can evaluate different options based on the borrower's actual circumstances.

Sometimes the best solution is a conventional loan.

Other times, it might be a jumbo loan, bank statement program, asset-based loan, or another specialized mortgage product.

My job isn't to force someone into a particular loan program.

It's to understand their financial situation, determine which programs they may qualify for, and help them compare the available options.

I've worked with plenty of borrowers who were told no somewhere else.

And in many of those situations, we were able to find a solution.

Not every time. But often enough that I think getting a second opinion is worth the effort.

Before You Give Up, Let's Take Another Look

If you've been turned down for a mortgage, don't automatically assume buying a home is off the table.

Find out exactly why the loan was denied. The CFPB recommends asking the lender for an explanation and reviewing your credit report for errors.

Then have someone who understands different mortgage programs review your situation.

You may discover that you have options your original lender never considered.

And even if you don't qualify today, a good loan officer should be able to explain what's preventing approval and what would need to change.

Just because one bank said no doesn't mean you can't buy a house.

Sometimes you just need someone willing to take a closer look.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a mortgage after being denied by another lender?

Yes. Different lenders have different underwriting guidelines and loan programs. Being denied by one lender doesn't automatically mean another lender will deny you. The reason for the original denial matters.

Can I qualify for a mortgage without using my tax returns?

Possibly. Certain non-QM mortgage programs allow self-employed borrowers to qualify using bank statements, profit-and-loss statements, or eligible assets instead of traditional tax-return income calculations.

Will a mortgage denial hurt my credit score?

The denial itself doesn't affect your credit score. However, the hard credit inquiry associated with the application may temporarily affect your score.

Can I get a mortgage if I'm self-employed and write off most of my income?

Potentially. Traditional mortgages generally rely on qualifying income calculated from tax returns. Alternative documentation programs may evaluate bank deposits, business cash flow, or other eligible financial resources.

How soon can I apply for a mortgage after being denied?

There's generally no mandatory waiting period simply because another lender denied your application. You can seek a second opinion immediately, although certain credit events or individual loan programs may have waiting-period requirements.
