Fixed vs. Adjustable Rate Mortgages — Detailed Reference

Reviewed November 15, 2026. Product terms and pricing vary by lender, borrower, property, and market conditions. Compare current written options with Adam →

A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the note rate unchanged. An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) has an initial rate period and later adjustments under the loan's index, margin, and caps. The better fit depends on the actual offers, expected holding period, and ability to handle the ARM's permitted payment changes.

Fixed vs. Adjustable Rate Mortgages

Understanding the key differences between these two mortgage types changes what you pay over the life of the loan.

On this page

What Is a Fixed-Rate Mortgage?

With a fixed-rate mortgage, the note rate and scheduled principal-and-interest payment stay the same for the loan term. The total monthly housing payment can still change when property taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, or escrow requirements change.

Fixed loans are offered in different terms. A shorter term generally amortizes principal faster but can require a higher monthly principal-and-interest payment than a longer term for the same balance. Compare actual rate, points, fees, monthly payment, and total interest rather than assuming one term is always cheaper.

What Is an Adjustable-Rate Mortgage?

An ARM keeps its initial interest rate for a stated period, then adjusts on the schedule in the note. Labels such as 5/1 or 5/6m describe the initial period and later adjustment frequency; structures vary, so read the Loan Estimate and loan documents for the exact schedule.

After the initial period, the rate is generally determined by the loan's index plus its margin, subject to applicable caps and any floor. SOFR is one common index, but the index is specific to the loan. A falling index does not guarantee that every ARM payment will fall.

Caps limit permitted rate changes at the first adjustment, later adjustments, and over the life of the loan. Compare those caps and ask for the highest possible payment. The initial ARM rate may be lower, similar to, or higher than a fixed-rate offer available at the same time, so compare written offers rather than relying on the loan label.

Pros and Cons Comparison

Here is a side-by-side look at the advantages and disadvantages of each mortgage type to help you evaluate which one aligns with your goals.

Factor Fixed-Rate Mortgage Adjustable-Rate Mortgage
Interest Rate Note rate stays the same for the loan term Initial rate period, then index plus margin subject to caps
Monthly Payment Principal and interest stay scheduled; taxes, insurance, and escrow can change Principal and interest may increase or decrease after the initial period
Risk Level No interest-rate adjustment risk, but other housing costs can change Future rate and payment changes must fit within the note's caps
Best For Borrowers who prioritize stable principal and interest Borrowers who understand the caps and can afford permitted increases
Rate Environment Compare current rate, points, fees, APR, caps, and payment scenarios; rate forecasts are not guarantees
Early Payoff / Refinance Compare upfront costs and break-even timing; selling or refinancing is not guaranteed

When Does Each Make Sense?

A fixed-rate mortgage may be worth prioritizing when:

  • You value a scheduled principal-and-interest payment that does not change.
  • The fixed offer's rate, points, fees, and break-even compare well with the ARM offers you actually received.
  • The ARM's maximum permitted payment would strain your budget.

An ARM may be worth comparing when:

  • The written ARM offer provides meaningful savings after points and fees during the initial period.
  • Your expected holding period is shorter than the initial period, while recognizing that a sale or refinance may not occur on schedule.
  • You understand the index, margin, adjustment frequency, caps, floor, and highest possible payment—and can afford the permitted increases.

How to Decide

The right choice depends on your personal financial situation, your timeline for staying in the home, and your tolerance for risk. There is no universally correct answer, and what works perfectly for one buyer may be wrong for another.

Start with three questions:

  1. What do the written offers show? Compare the same loan amount and lock period, including rate, points, lender credits, APR, fees, and principal-and-interest payment.
  2. What can change? For an ARM, identify the index, margin, first adjustment date, adjustment frequency, caps, floor, and highest possible payment.
  3. What if the plan changes? Test the cost if you keep the loan longer than expected or cannot refinance when planned.
