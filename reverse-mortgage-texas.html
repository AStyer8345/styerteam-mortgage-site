Reverse Mortgage Texas: HECM and Proprietary Options

Compare the loan, ongoing obligations, costs, and alternatives before using home equity.

A reverse mortgage lets an eligible homeowner borrow against home equity while keeping title. The most common option is an FHA-insured Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM) for homeowners age 62 or older. Proprietary reverse mortgages are separate private products and are not federally insured. What should I know about this option?

How Does a Reverse Mortgage Work in Texas?

With an FHA-insured HECM, an eligible homeowner generally does not make a required monthly principal-and-interest payment. Interest and fees are added to the balance, so the amount owed usually grows and remaining equity usually declines. The homeowner must keep the home as a principal residence, pay property charges such as taxes and homeowners insurance, and maintain the property.

HECMs are FHA-insured and follow federal rules. Proprietary reverse mortgages are private products that are not federally insured; age, property value, proceeds, costs, counseling, payment options, and borrower protections vary by investor. Compare the written loan terms and alternatives rather than assuming the HECM rules apply to every reverse mortgage.

Hypothetical Example • Not an Approval Austin (Northwest Hills), Texas

Compare the full housing payment, closing costs, and loan terms using a current written quote.

The Situation: An Austin couple (ages 71 and 69) owned a home in Northwest Hills valued at $900,000 with a $180,000 remaining balance on a forward mortgage, requiring a $2,400/month payment that strained their Social Security and pension income.

The Comparison: A current HECM proposal would need to show the principal limit, mandatory payoff of the existing lien, closing costs, mortgage-insurance charges, interest rate, remaining funds or credit-line availability, and the effect of future balance growth.

The Decision: Paying off the forward mortgage could remove its required monthly principal-and-interest payment, but taxes, insurance, maintenance, occupancy, and other property charges would continue. The household should compare that cash-flow change with the rising reverse-mortgage balance, reduced future equity, heirs' plans, and alternatives.

Disclosure: Hypothetical education only. It is not a loan approval, current quote, tax or legal advice, or a recommendation. Eligibility, proceeds, rates, costs, and obligations depend on the borrower, property, counseling, appraisal, program, and written lender terms.

Clear Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Straightforward answers to the questions Central Texas borrowers ask most.

Does the bank own my home with a reverse mortgage?

The title remains in the homeowner's name, while the reverse mortgage creates a lien against the property. Selling the home or transferring it through an estate requires the loan to be resolved under its written terms.

What happens to my home when I pass away?

For an FHA-insured HECM, heirs may keep the home by satisfying the loan, sell it and retain any equity after the debt and selling costs, or turn it over to the lender. If the HECM balance exceeds the home's value, CFPB guidance explains the 95-percent-of-appraised-value option. Coborrower and eligible non-borrowing-spouse rules can change the timing.

What are my obligations under a reverse mortgage?

HECM borrowers must use the home as their principal residence, pay property charges such as taxes and homeowners insurance on time, and keep the home in good repair. Failure to meet these obligations can lead to default or foreclosure. Proprietary loan requirements depend on the written program terms.

How should reverse mortgage proceeds and benefits be reviewed?

Reverse mortgage advances are loan proceeds rather than wages, but tax and public-benefit effects depend on the borrower's circumstances and how funds are held or used. Review the plan with a qualified tax adviser and the applicable benefit administrator.

How does the HECM line of credit growth feature work?

An adjustable-rate FHA HECM can include a credit-line growth feature that increases the amount available to borrow, up to the maximum stated in the mortgage. It is not interest earned in a deposit account. Proprietary products may work differently, so compare the written terms.

Who may qualify for an FHA-insured HECM reverse mortgage?

HECM borrowers are generally age 62 or older, use an eligible primary residence, complete required counseling, and must satisfy financial assessment and property requirements.

Does the homeowner still pay taxes and insurance?

Yes. The homeowner remains responsible for property taxes, homeowners insurance, maintenance, and other applicable property charges. Failure to meet obligations can cause the loan to become due.

When does a reverse mortgage become due?

A reverse mortgage generally becomes due after the last eligible borrower dies, sells the home, permanently moves out, or fails to meet loan obligations. Exact terms depend on the loan.

What alternatives should be compared?

Depending on the goal, compare a traditional refinance, HELOC, home-equity loan, asset-depletion mortgage, retirement distributions, selling or downsizing, or using other assets.
