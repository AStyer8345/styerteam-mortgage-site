What should I know about this option?

How Do Underwriters Evaluate Schedule K-1 Income?

When you own 25% or more of an S-Corporation or Partnership, mortgage underwriters evaluate both your individual tax return (Form 1040 Schedule E) and the underlying corporate return (Form 1120S or 1065). Simply showing positive ordinary business income on Box 1 of your Schedule K-1 is not always enough.

Under agency guidelines, lenders perform a liquidity and distribution test: they confirm that either (1) actual cash distributions were paid to you matching the K-1 income, or (2) the business has sufficient positive cash flow, liquid working capital, and solvency to support distributions without jeopardizing operations. Additionally, non-cash deductions such as depreciation, depletion, and amortization can be added back to increase your qualifying income.

Illustrative Case • Law Firm Partner Austin (Downtown / Clarksville), Texas

Clarksville Law Firm Partner — $1.1M Home with W-2 + K-1 Distributions

The Situation: A partner in a downtown Austin litigation firm wanted to purchase a $1,100,000 historic home in Clarksville with 20% down ($220,000). They received a base W-2 salary of $120,000 plus an annual Schedule K-1 profit distribution averaging $260,000.

The Challenge: A retail bank loan officer only counted the $120,000 W-2 salary, stating that because K-1 distributions fluctuated ($230,000 in Year 1 and $290,000 in Year 2), the K-1 income could not be used. The buyer was told their maximum loan was capped at $600,000.

The Wholesale Solution: Adam audited the firm's Form 1065 partnership returns. The firm had over $1.5M in liquid operating reserves and demonstrated an 8-year track record of consistent distributions. Under agency guidelines, Adam averaged the 2-year K-1 distributions ($260,000) and combined it with the $120,000 W-2 salary for total qualifying income of $380,000/year ($31,666/month). The $880,000 jumbo loan was approved with a 22% DTI and closed in 19 days.

Disclosure: Illustrative scenario based on composite borrower profile. Actual interest rates, loan limits, and qualification guidelines depend on credit profile, property appraisal, and lender criteria.

Clear Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Straightforward answers to the questions self-employed borrowers ask most.

Do I need both my personal and business tax returns to qualify with K-1 income?

If you own 25% or more of the business entity, yes. Lenders require two years of personal returns (Form 1040) and two years of corporate returns (Form 1120S for S-Corps or Form 1065 for Partnerships), including all K-1 schedules.

What happens if my business has income on Box 1 of the K-1 but did not distribute cash?

If the business did not distribute cash, the underwriter must verify that the business has adequate liquidity and working capital to support distributions. If the business is capital-constrained, underwriters may decline to count the undistributed K-1 income.

Can depreciation from the corporate return be added back to my income?

Yes! Non-cash deductions on the corporate return—including Form 1120S/1065 depreciation, depletion, amortization, and one-time extraordinary losses—can be added back proportionate to your ownership percentage.

Can I use business bank funds to pay my down payment and closing costs?

Yes, provided the business funds are in an account where you are an authorized signer. Underwriters require an accountant letter or a liquidity analysis confirming that withdrawing funds will not adversely affect daily business operations.

What if my K-1 income declined in the most recent tax year?

If K-1 earnings declined, underwriters will typically use the lower, most recent year's earnings rather than a two-year average. If the decline is severe, an explanation and year-to-date P&L may be required to prove stability.
